\section{SURD components in the five historical NGC~5548 seasons}
\label{sec:epoch_surd_results}

\subsection{Series, epochs and analysis design}

We apply the seasonal strategy of \citet{Lu2022} and \citet{Xi2025} to the
historical monitoring of NGC~5548 between 1988 and 1993. Their seasons are
analysed independently; we do the same for the five historical seasons, which
carry the labels Year~1 to Year~5 in Table~6 of \citet{Lu2022}. The epochs are the
intervals of Table~2 of \citet{Peterson2004ApJ}: JD less 2400000 from 47509
through 47809 (1989), 47861 through 48179 (1990), 48225 through 48534 (1991),
48623 through 48898 (1992) and 48954 through 49255 (1993).

Five observed series are prepared for each epoch: the 5100~\AA\ continuum
(F5100), the total broad H$\beta$ flux, and its blue, core and red components.
The line measurements are the corrected published columns of
\citet{WandersPeterson1996}, with blue, core and red defined over the observed
wavelength intervals 4870--4920, 4920--4960 and 4960--5010~\AA. These fixed
wavelength intervals play the role of the equal flux velocity bins that
\citet{Xi2025} construct over 4700--5050~\AA; they are not equal flux bins.
Observations on the same date are combined by their mean. The continuum retains
its host galaxy contribution. Total and component fluxes come from the same
spectra, and the total is the sum of its components, so they are not four
independent measurements.

Two variable sets are analysed, each with four members: total, blue, core and
red H$\beta$; and F5100, blue, core and red H$\beta$. Each member becomes the
future target $Y$ in turn. All four members, including the past of the target
itself, are the predictors $\mathbf{X}$. The unchanged SURD implementation of
\citet{MartinezSanchez2024SURD} is used throughout. For four predictors the decomposition has
four unique components $U$, eleven redundant components $R$ (six pairs, four
triples and one set of four predictors) and eleven synergistic components $S$
with the same combinations. These are 26 components rather than 25. Following
Figure~4.1 of \citet{MartinezSanchez2025Thesis}, each component is divided by the joint mutual
information between the future target and the whole predictor vector,
\begin{equation}
 f_a(\tau)=\frac{I_a(Y;\mathbf{X}_{\tau})}{I(Y;\mathbf{X}_{\tau})},
 \qquad \sum_{a=1}^{26}f_a(\tau)=1 ,
 \label{eq:ep_component_normalization}
\end{equation}
where $I_a$ is the contribution of component $a$ averaged over the states of the
target (equations 3.16 of the thesis, ordered as in equation 3.17). The causality
leak of equation 3.6 is normalized by the entropy of the target,
\begin{equation}
 \ell(\tau)=\frac{H(Y\mid\mathbf{X}_{\tau})}{H(Y)} ,
 \label{eq:ep_leakage}
\end{equation}
and shown in its own panel. The component sums equal one to within
$1.6\times10^{-15}$ in all 7200 decompositions.

\paragraph{Regular grid and lags.} Every series is linearly interpolated onto a
daily grid that spans its epoch. Interpolation never extrapolates and never
bridges a gap between neighbouring observations longer than 15 days. The target
is $Y(t)$ and the predictors are $X_i(t-L-\delta_i)$, with base lag $L$ from 1
through 30 days in one day steps. The 30 day span covers the common lag ranges
proposed for the modern seasons. Probabilities are histograms with two bins and,
as a sensitivity setting, three bins. Bin boundaries are the marginal quantiles
of each full native epoch series. Table~\ref{tab:ep_support} lists the data
support. Every lag of every curve retains at least 118 tuples. Grid tuples are
interpolated and autocorrelated, so they are not independent samples.

\paragraph{Uncommon predictor lags (equation 3.18).} Equation 3.18 of the thesis
builds the predictor vector from copies of each variable at different past
times. We give every predictor variable its own lag $L+\delta_i$, so that the four
predictors still define the 26 components. The common lag analysis has
$\delta_i=0$. In the \emph{blue leading} configuration the blue wing is sampled
$\delta=5$ days further back and in the \emph{red leading} configuration the red
wing is. The component that responds earlier to a continuum event carries that
event further in its past, so this is the arrangement that aligns the wings
under the two kinematic patterns that \citet{Xi2025} describe. The five day
offset is a stipulated analysis choice of the size of the uncertainties of
published H$\beta$ lags (two to three days); it is not a measured delay. Every
configuration and lag is analysed for both variable sets, all four targets and all
five epochs: 7200 decompositions, 120 figures.

\textbf{Leakage.} In 36 of 40 curves the leakage minimum is at the shortest lag of
one day, where the history of the target itself predicts its own future. The
exceptions are the blue and core targets of the F5100 set in 1989 (minima at 8 and
24 days, leakage 0.12 and 0.05), the total target of the total set in 1990 (3 days)
and the core target of the F5100 set in 1991, whose leakage reaches zero to numerical
precision at 4 days. The leakage maximum lies at 20 days or longer in 35 of 40
curves. Median minimum and maximum leakage are 0.12 and 0.70 for the F5100 set and
0.09 and 0.89 for the total set. The leakage at the largest lags of the total set
approaches the 0.86 to 0.98 that independent categorical variables produce in the
calibration below for 90 to 512 tuples. Little predictive information remains at
such lags relative to the bias level of the estimator.

\textbf{Largest components.} At the leakage minimum the largest component is the
four-way redundancy $R(H,B,C,R)$ in 16 of the 20 curves of the total set: the
components share the same spectra and sum to the total, so their past states carry
the same information about the future. In the F5100 set it is a unique component
(11 curves, mostly $U(F)$ and the target's own history) or a redundancy among the
line components (9 curves). At the leakage maximum the largest component is a
synergy in 11 of 20 total set curves and a unique component in five; in the F5100 set
it is $U(F)$ in 10 curves and a synergy in seven. The largest component then carries
on average 0.32 (total set) and 0.36 (F5100 set) of the joint information, against
0.55 and 0.57 at the leakage minimum.

\textbf{Continuum to line information.} The F5100 set isolates what the continuum
adds to a line target beyond the line components: the unique term $U(F)$. For the
blue, core and red targets it rises to a maximum of 0.29 to 0.87 (two bins) at lags
of 16 to 29 days in 13 of 15 curves (Table~\ref{tab:ep_lu}). The two exceptions
have no continuum term above 0.05: the core target in 1991 and the red target in
1993. With three bins the exceptions are the blue and core targets in 1991. A unique continuum component that
peaks at a lag of tens of days and leaves leakage near its minimum (0.05 for the
1989 core target at 24 days) is what a delayed response to the continuum would
produce. The same peaks give a velocity ordering of the response that we compare
with the published seasons below.

\textbf{Epoch comparison.} Mean leakage over lags and targets (common lags, two bins)
is 0.49, 0.53, 0.43, 0.58 and 0.65 for 1989 to 1993. These follow the ordering of
the grid tuple counts (minimum 134, 161, 133, 211 and 231, respectively), with rank
correlation one. Our categorical calibration shows that fewer tuples give a lower
estimated leakage even for independent variables, so differences of leakage between
epochs mainly reflect sample size.

Sampling the blue wing five days further back lowers the mean leakage relative to
common lags in eight of the ten epoch and set combinations; the red wing does so in
four (1989 and 1990), and raises it in 1991 to 1993. The mean differences are small,
at most 0.08, but the allocation among components changes substantially: the median
total variation $\frac12\sum_a|f_a^{\rm uncommon}-f_a^{\rm common}|$ is 0.30 for both
configurations (0.23 to 0.24 for the F5100 set and 0.36 to 0.40 for the total set),
and ranges from 0.22 in 1993 to 0.46 in 1989. The lag assigned to each predictor
therefore matters for which components receive the information even when the total
information changes little. Blue leading gives the lower mean leakage for all three line
targets in 1991 and 1992 (both sets) and in the F5100 set in 1990 and 1993; in 1989 it is
higher for most targets. This test uses all targets, including the continuum
and the target's own history, so it is not a velocity resolved delay measurement.

The median peak lag over the detected line targets is 23, 27, 20.5, 17 and 23 days
(two bins) against the published 19.7, 18.6, 15.9, 11.0 and 13.0 days. It exceeds
the published lag in every season by a median of 6.0 days (5.3 days with three
bins). The rank correlation between the two sets of lags is 0.67 for two bins and
0.36 for three (five points, not significant). Both show shorter lags in 1991 and
1992 than in 1989 and 1990, and a longer lag in 1993 than in 1992 (17 to 23 days for
two bins, 19 to 20 days for three bins). A maximum of a unique information fraction on a one day grid is
not the centroid of a cross-correlation function; the information term also
depends on the history of the target and on the broad transfer function, and a
longer apparent lag is expected when the response is spread over time. The comparison
therefore supports a continuum driven response with a delay of the right order in
the bright historical state, not an independent lag measurement.

\citet{Xi2025} measure much shorter H$\beta$ lags in the modern low luminosity
seasons (Table~\ref{tab:ep_xi}) and show that the velocity resolved lag profile
changes between them: an M-shaped profile in 2015, a red-leads-blue pattern in 2019
and 2021 interpreted as inflow, and a blue-leads-red pattern in 2020 and 2023
interpreted as outflow. We read the same ordering from the peak lags of
Table~\ref{tab:ep_lu}. With two bins the red wing peaks earlier than the blue wing in
1989 (20 against 27 d), 1991 (18 against 23 d) and 1992 (16 against 21 d), the core
peaks earlier than the blue wing in 1993 (20 against 26 d), and the order is reversed
in 1990 (blue 23, core 27, red 29 d). With three bins the red peak is again earlier
in 1989, 1992 and 1993, and is tied with the blue peak in 1990. A red-leads-blue
ordering is therefore found in four of the five historical seasons and survives the
change of bins in 1989, 1992 and 1993, similar to the signature that
\citet{Xi2025} identify in 2019 and 2021; the season 1990 resembles their
blue-leads-red seasons but is not robust to the bin count. The differences between
wings are five to seven days, a handful of grid steps, and have no uncertainty.
They do not by themselves establish radial inflow: the Wanders and Peterson
intervals are fixed wavelength bands and not equal flux bins, the three line
series share spectra, and the uncommon lag test of the previous subsection does
not single out one wing ordering. The velocity ordering is nevertheless the
information theoretic counterpart of the quantity on which \citet{Xi2025} base
their kinematic classification, and it varies between seasons in our data as in
theirs.

The historical lags of 11 to 20 days \citep{Lu2022} are longer than the 7 to 10
days of 2015 to 2021 and comparable with the 15.6 days of 2023. \citet{Xi2025}
show that the broad line region radius follows the optical luminosity with a delay of
3.5 years: the radius fell with the luminosity after the decline that reached its
minimum around 2005 and 2010, and approached its typical historical value only by
2023. The short lags of 2015 to 2021 therefore belong to a contracted line region
that has not yet recovered, and the historical seasons correspond to the typical
radius to which it returned. At the lag of the continuum peak, the leakage of the
line targets remains between 0.15 and 0.71 (median 0.54, two bins), so about half of
the entropy of a line target is not accounted for by the monitored observables. A
driving continuum that differs from the observed 5100~\AA\ flux, as in the disc wind
shielding proposed for the 2014 `BLR holiday' (cited in the introduction of
\citealt{Xi2025}), is one possible reason; the noise of the fluxes and the coarse two and three state description are
others.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the number of bins}

Changing the histograms from two to three bins changes the allocation substantially
(Table~\ref{tab:ep_bins}). The median total variation $D_{\rm TV}=\frac12\sum_a|f_a^{(2)}-f_a^{(3)}|$
between the bin settings is between 0.27 and 0.58 in all epochs and
configurations. For common lags the mean leakage is lower for three bins (0.34 and 0.46
for the F5100 and total sets against 0.46 and 0.61), consistent with the larger finite
sample information of sparse histograms. The position of the main peaks can move by
ten days or more (Table~\ref{tab:ep_lu}), so the quantities that depend on a single
peak lag should be read with this resolution.

\subsection{Limits of the interpretation}

The component fractions describe how the available predictive information is
distributed; they do not by themselves identify causal coupling or a physical delay.
The independent categorical benchmark below shows that finite samples of
independent variables produce substantial apparent information, particularly with
three states, and that leakage is biased low at small sample sizes. Observation
dates, interpolation across the gaps, flux errors, and the shared spectra of the
line series are not included in that benchmark. Lag choices ($1$--$30$ d, five day
offsets) and the 15 day interpolation limit are fixed in advance and were not
tuned. No significance level has been computed for the scans.
